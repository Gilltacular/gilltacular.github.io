ISIS: The Terror We Created

ISIS, a terrorist organization that now controls large parts of Northern Iraq. Born of the broken remnants of al Qaeda, this violent regime grew to power in a time of political and religious unrest after American troops withdrew from the country. A growing death toll leaves a blood red trail across Iraq. What facilitated their success where other regimes have failed and who is to blame? The United States government is responsible for creating the climate in Iraq which ISIS used to grow and succeed without opposition.

"The United States government remained silent despite being advised of the dissent stirring under their chosen Iraqi leader’s special brand of authoritarian rule that left thousands of Sunni dead."

Iraq starts to fall apart as the United States military pulls out of the country, leaving in charge Prime Minister Nuri al-Maliki. Almost immediately after Iraq was standing on its own two feet, after nearly eight years of American occupation, dissention stirs as Prime Minister Maliki orders the detainment and arrest of Vice President Tariq al-Hashemi under charges of involvement in a terrorist plot against Shia targets. Throughout the next year Maliki reportedly detained thousands of Sunni who were held without charge, many of which were executed by militia and private security. David Kilcullen, former U.S. Military advisor explains how Maliki continued to make matters worse, “Sunni leaders in the army and Sunni leaders in the police began to be sidelined, and people with strong Shia sectarian bent replaced them. And that meant that a lot of people felt they were being excluded. And that was true, they were” (Smith, 2014). Some believe these were acts of paranoia grown from a brutal past in which Maliki’s close relatives were tortured under Saddam’s rule (Smith, 2014). The United States government remained silent despite being advised of the dissent stirring under their chosen Iraqi leader’s special brand of authoritarian rule that left thousands of Sunni dead. Instead they chose to wash their hands of the matter, stating that the decisions of the prime minister are an internal Iraqi matter. New Yorker writer Dexter Filkins believes the U.S. government was wrong in their stance and should have given a more weight of responsibility to the matter. According to Filkins (2004), “we destroyed the Iraqi state and then we rebuilt this thing - this kind of rickety thing that doesn't work very well. And then we left, you know, and now it doesn't work at all”, a statement that illustrates just how fragile Iraq was at the time. Taking the U.S. government’s silence as affirmation, Maliki’s decisions marked the start of a series of events that would shape the hostility to come.

The air was wrought with frustration as the Sunni populace started to protest against Maliki's abuse. The tensions reach the ears of White House officials. James Jeffrey, former U.S. Ambassador to Iraq states, “This was a constant warning that I had made and that others had made before me, that Maliki was a problem. On the other hand, the president and the country had taken the position Iraq was a mistake, we’ve ended our war in Iraq”(Smith, 2014). Former Secretary of Defense Leon Panetta agrees with Jeffrey stating that, “You’ve got to continue to put pressure on them to do the right thing. I think everybody just kept their fingers crossed that, ultimately, Maliki would somehow step down or be replaced and that Iraq would be in a better place” (Smith, 2014). Panetta and Jeffrey were not the only ones who asked the U.S. government to take incoming intelligence seriously. Smith (2014) explains how U.S. Ambassador to Syria Robert Ford urged the President to help, “having al Qaeda or al Qaeda-affiliated groups able to operate freely would be as much a risk to the United States as Somalia, Yemen and Afghanistan were. And in each of those places, the Americans had to act”; the President however, chose to pursue no action in the matter. Though many may argue over President Obama’s decision, the fact remains that without military involvement the Syrian rebels were not successful in holding off al Qaeda attacks. Attacks that won al Qaeda bolstered ranks and the resources they needed to expand into Iraq. Smith (2014) reports that soon after, in March 2013, symbols started to point toward a new al Qaeda uprising as black flags appeared at the Ramadi protests. He explains that, “around this time, they started calling themselves the Islamic State in Iraq and al Sham—ISIS” and, “Their presence stoked Maliki’s worst fears” (Smith, 2014).

"We may not like them, but we need them because otherwise, there’s nothing. Nobody’s going to protect us. And the Americans aren’t here anymore."

A month later, in April 2013, the Sunni grew tired of their protests falling on deaf ears. During the height of Sunni protests in Hawika, a confrontation broke out in which some police officers were killed. Maliki moves with overwhelming response, attributing the killings to al Qaeda terrorism. He issues tanks, military personnel, and heavy equipment to be brought in to quell the protest and by the end of the day the bodies of hundreds of protesters line the streets. The newly founded ISIS capitalized on the Sunni people’s anger and grief, urging them to put away peaceful protest and take up arms. Filkins suggests this was an obvious response to the situation, “If you take Iraq’s Sunni Community, its leadership, it’s full of reasonable people. It’s full of secular, educated, very moderate people. But they look around and they say, ‘Where do we go? The only people that are going to protect us are these really hard guys. We may not like them, but we need them because otherwise, there’s nothing. Nobody’s going to protect us. And the Americans aren’t here anymore’”(Smith, 2014). Pennetta expands on the situation with his own view when he explains that, “What happened here is that by virtue of -- of the Shia not opening it up and allowing Sunnis to participate, they created the monster that has led to ISIS” (Smith, 2014).

As the United States continued to ignore the growing terrorist threat, ISIS began planning for an attack on the second largest city in Iraq, Mosul. Intelligence of the attack reached Washington who again turned a blind eye to the information explains Jeffrey, “The administration not only was warned by everybody back in January, it actually announced that it was going to intensify its support against ISIS with the Iraqi armed forces. And it did almost nothing.”(Smith, 2014). On June 6th suicide car bombers wracked downtown Mosul. The Iraqi army collapsed deserting the city and leaving it in the hands of ISIS. By June 10th, a mere four days later, a group of 800 ISIS militants had secured Mosul, Iraq, and their population of 1.8 million people. They also gained a hoard of U.S. made military equipment, tanks, and heavy armored vehicles that were stored within. This equipment they then used to take multiple other cities. Ali Khedry, a former U.S. Embassy Sr. Advisor states just how big of a boon this was for ISIS, “I don’t think bin Laden could’ve ever dreamt that elements even more radical than his own al Qaeda would be armed with American M1-A1 tanks or 155-millimeter artillery or up-armored Humvees or MRAPs.”(Smith, 2014), but armed they were. It was not long before ISIS had the support to declare themselves the religious successor to Muhammad, giving them the power to take the pulpit of the great Mosque and give a jihadist sermon that would rouse thousands to join support for their cause out of religious obligation. ISIS, now seemingly against no opposition, at the height of its recruitment, armed with American equipment, and growing faster than they could have ever imagined, went on to take over the Kurdish Peshmerga, reputed as Iraq’s fiercest military command. Northern Iraq was in shambles.

“I think President Obama has a fundamental belief that any military action or aiding local fighters will lead to almost inexorably 150,000 troops on the ground like Iraq, or 500,000 like Vietnam—slippery slope, down the drain, huge disaster for America. I think he believes that sincerely. I think he’s absolutely wrong”

It is not until Kurdistan came under attack that American military involvement began. Khedry sheds some light on why Kurdistan was the line in the sand for U.S. government, “Kurdistan is the silver lining of Iraq. A trillion dollars’ worth of global energy companies -- Total, Chevron, Exxon, and Gazprom Neft -- are invested in Kurdistan”(Smith, 2014), insinuating that its role as a hub for American investments and energy interests is the key factor. Citing President Obama’s declaration that, “America should use force, unilaterally if necessary, only when its core interests are threatened.”, the Economist article “‘Iraq is not going to be a perfect place’” (2014) suggests that what constitutes an “American interest” may be too vague to be considered a reliable measure of intent. The article further points out that, “The capture of several big cities by people who have invented a new online genre, the war crime self-portrait, does not yet cross the threshold”

The current leaders within U.S. government still feel they should not intervene. Former Ambassador Jeffrey maintains that this is the wrong choice stating, “I think President Obama has a fundamental belief that any military action or aiding local fighters will lead to almost inexorably 150,000 troops on the ground like Iraq, or 500,000 like Vietnam—slippery slope, down the drain, huge disaster for America. I think he believes that sincerely. I think he’s absolutely wrong” (Smith, 2014). On the other hand, Deputy National Security Advisor Ben Rhodes reinforces the U.S. government's stance on the matter arguing that, “The president’s view is that we do not need to do this with U.S. combat forces on the ground”; a stance that recent historical events in Iraq would dictate is incorrect. The recent success of ISIS is indirectly caused by an opportunity gap created by the inaction to warnings and evidence that the U.S. has been presented with throughout this conflict. This is further made worse by the hostile and religiously biased rule of Iraq’s former Prime Minister. “Suddenly, you have a complete breakdown of the state in Syria.” explains Filkins, “You have this vast, open space between the two countries. And so these guys, they’re suddenly able to find life” (Smith, 2014). The American part in this war was one of inaction. A part that facilitated the conditions with which ISIS was able to prosper. As John Stuart Mill (1867) reminds us, “Bad men need nothing more to compass their ends, than that good men should look on and do nothing” (p.36).
