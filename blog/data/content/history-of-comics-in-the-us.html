History of Comics in the U.S.

Long before Spiderman, Batman, and the X-Men, comic books existed in some form or another. As the Random History website (2008) states, “The thematic elements of the genre can arguably be traced back to ancient Greek mythological gods and superheroes”. It was not until 1938 that they were turned into a bustling industry. This year marked the introduction of Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster’s character Superman and marked what is referred to now as the ‘Golden Age of Comics’. Superman quickly became popular and many other superheroes followed in his success. Such names as Wonder Woman, Captain America, The Flash, Batman, and the Green Lantern were introduced to the world during this time and unlike the short daily strips in the newspapers before them. featured ‘cinematic storytelling over several pages, using a montage of light, sound, dynamic framing, and vibrant colors (RandomHistory, 2008).

Television created competition for comic books as the nation came out of World War II. This coupled with a growing worry that the comic books would corrupt the young minds of those who read them and influence them to commit the violence sometimes depicted within them led to the establishment of the Comics Code Authority as a regulatory body to soothe the mind of the populace at the time and gain back their readers.

The way comic books changed over time reflected the way the populace of the country viewed certain topics being handled within their pages. For instance, during WWII the thematic material within comics was more patriotic and as Random History (2008) states was, “used to build consensus and morale”. Nearing the height of the Vietnam war however, in a time where the United States had a much different view of itself and it’s involvements abroad the characters began to take on weaknesses, flaws, or defects. These characters were often considered misunderstood, outcasts, nerds, or outsiders and directly reflected the public views and internal struggles at the time. Characters such as Spiderman and the Hulk are perfect examples of this age where comics and the shifting U.S. ideal became more than just fights of good versus evil, starring relatable and sometimes even virtuous villains as well as selfish innately flawed heroes. These are the characteristics marked the ‘Silver Age of Comics’.

Similar changes that reflected the common view of the country have continued throughout the 1970’s and 80’s during what is referred to as the ‘Bronze Age of Comics’ when the country was broadening its acceptance of what artistic and stylistic tastes it had and again in the 1980’s for the ‘Iron Age of Comics’ when the storytelling became more sophisticated and characters often had multiple views of themselves and struggled as much internally as they did physically.

As we look back at the different ages of comics and the storytelling within their pages, we can see a bit of ourselves from the past. These small stories showcase common beliefs and opinions of times we can never experience again and share the horrors and triumphs of the people who survived through such events. Comic books have influenced our culture greatly throughout their history. As Martin (2009) states,”Just as literary adaptation provided an important stage in the commercialization of film, so a growing number of prose classics are being adapted into the medium of comics”. The advent of comic books has inspired art, culture, and movies in the same ways such storytelling did for the ancient Greeks; by creating the shared ideal of humanity we all hope to be in the time we live in.
