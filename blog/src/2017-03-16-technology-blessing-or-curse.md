“But to what end have people used this power? For good or ill? Enhancement or destruction? Pleasure or torment? These are the ultimate questions that a history of technology must ask” (Cowan, 1997). With this simple quote Cowan describes the inherent difficulty with labeling technology as wholly either a blessing or a curse.

Inherent to history is the idea that what is done cannot be undone. While a new discovery can change the landscape of how we understand something in science and language changes based on the social norms of the time, history will always be history and its facts will never change. It cannot be either a blessing or a curse as it is simply the product of our work with it. Without humanity innovation would not exist as nothing natural ever innovates. Yes, it adapts, evolves and changes, but it never creates something entirely different than it had before in order to improve upon its process or advance itself as a whole.

This fact rests the responsibility of innovation solely in our hands. As the creators of these advancements we are responsible for the outcomes of its good and bad effects and we must balance that innovation with the natural world in order to coexist within it and not destroy ourselves. The “blessings” of technology are easy to foresee when something new is introduced, but it is on us to also see the “curses” and plan a way to strike a happy medium between them in a way that encourages our health as a populace.

References:

Cowan, S. R. (1997). A Social History of American Technology. New York, NY: Oxford University Press
