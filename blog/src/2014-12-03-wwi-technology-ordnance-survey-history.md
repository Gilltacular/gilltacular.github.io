One of the great innovations to come out of World War I was the Ordnance Survey. The Ordnance Survey is an executive agency of the Government of the United Kingdom that is one of the world's largest producers of maps. Before 1915 the French maps that the British had were inaccurate, out of date, and used scales that were too small for the applications needed.

Maps, as you can probably imagine, are extremely important in a war. They denote the geographical boons and conditions that allow strategy in placement of troops and artillery as well as mark the lines and distances of allied troops in order to ensure their safety from friendly fire.

By 1915 over twenty thousand maps were being created each day; the Ordnance Survey supplying over ninety percent of all maps used in France (Gemma, 2011). These maps outlined trench lines and geography using aerial photography and ground units with the use of ‘flash spotting’ and ‘sound ranging’. According to BBC (2014), “Ordnance Survey officers and the Field Survey Units of the Royal Engineers refined techniques to direct fire onto enemy guns. 'Flash-spotting’ and ‘sound-ranging’ involved the recording of the flash or report of gunfire at triangulation points, enabling Survey Officers to plot the guns’ location”. The Daily post (2011), quotes a sapper from the Royal Engineers’ Air Photographic interpretation unit as he described his job:

> “... we did all the photographic stuff for the Ardennes - an officer would get a target coming in and I had to look up where it actually was and plot a route in for the aircraft - we had different maps and you could tell where the anti-aircraft guns were. I used to make a photograph so they could see what was in front of them - we had great success. It is amazing - a photograph taken at 25,000 feet and you could tell where somebody had walked in a field, the footsteps through the grass".

Throughout the war sixty-seven Ordnance Survey staff lost their lives, but supplies nearly thirty-three million maps, plans, and diagrams to the forces” (Gemma, 2011). This work continued even until after World War II, printing near three hundred and forty-two million maps for allied forces. The Ordnance Survey was the main supplier of maps to the battlefields in Europe and north west Africa, despite the bombing of their Southampton London Road main office.
