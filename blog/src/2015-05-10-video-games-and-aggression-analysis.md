In any environment where we are able to make connections to things outside of ourselves, we will. We, as beings of self preservation, find it necessary to search for the commonality we have to other things. It is integral to us feeling as if we belong in the environment and our construction of our identity over time. These connections though can easily serve another purpose as well, taking us from an approach of self focused betterment to the extreme illogical nature of making connections where there are none. We can force pieces of the puzzle together in such a way that the picture changes, but in doing so we never really get at the truth of what the whole picture was suppose to be, instead creating something else entirely. I believe this is what we have done in the common view of how society sees video games and their link to aggressive traits.

In order to characterize any link between two things we must first define what those things are. In this case, we can start by defining aggression. Social Psychology defines aggression as “any action intended to hurt another person”, giving aggression an understood commonality with anger, frustration, competition, and violence. Video gaming in general is understood to be defined in the same fashion as any other game in that it can take on a variety of roles from storytelling to competitive combat. It is however unique in its delivery of this content, usually blending pieces of roles together to hit a wider audience than one or the other would by themselves.

Freud (as cited in Social Psychology, 2006, p. 406) recognized that aggression was an instinctive behavior inherent in everyone, but that each person had the option of where to focus it. Some, he theorized, turned their aggression outwardly while in others that same focus could be directed on themselves. In this vein of thought aggression can be viewed as a good or a bad thing depending on the severity, type, and intent of the aggressive action. A competitive nature between accepted rivals, for instance, can encourage each to do their best and put pressure on the individuals to succeed, while the extremes of such competition could lead to development of violence and hate between the two rivals. In this assessment the power lies in the decisions and actions of the individual to decide which outcome, good or bad, comes to fruition. I would argue that it is no different in video games or any other type of outlet where an aggressive model can be applied.

Video games in themselves traditionally do in fact offer a stunning variety of aggressive examples. Competitive types of aggression that is the backbone of the player versus player (PVP) aspect some games are built around, violent aggression in the portrayal and execution of conflict in stories and events games focus on, and even examples of inward aggression stemming from the inability to perform to the level required by difficulty of the game. These forms of aggression are existent in all the general forms of entertainment and media we share and I would venture to say that it has entered into the realm of social norm just as it has with those other forms.

>Through video games we are able to metaphorically exist in a particular role and experience a level of near social interaction with the ‘actors’ of the game to learn about a greater message or accomplish a task. We even can fulfill group social needs in some games through our interactions with other players. All of these elements of gaming, as well as many more not shared in the scope of this paper, share the potential for aggressive behavior by the users and in some cases programmed into the non playable characters/actors (NPCs).

Aggression is inherent in games because it is inherent in humanity and those who create games wish to draw the user of the game close to the characters they have created by making them resemble human themselves, much in the same way that a writer of a great novel would. It is not just aggression that shares this idea though. Many human traits are exemplified in gaming, such as sadness, joy, ambition, heroism, affection, sexuality, violence, hate, moral stance, and these themes are all elements of the overarching message that is being conveyed. These ideas and messages have no more power than the player gives them in the same way that an marketing can influence what we look at, but can not make our decision to actually buy their products. Dr. Christopher Ferguson, a Professor of Behavioral, Applied Sciences and Criminal Justice at A&M University, drives this point home with his quote on the findings of his research into the matter:
>“We found that depressed mood and association with delinquent peers were the strongest and most consistent risk factors for youth violence across outcome measures. Parents' use of verbal cruelty in domestic relationships and the child's antisocial personality traits were also reasonably strong predictors of violent behavior. By contrast video game violence exposure and television violence exposure were not found to be predictors of youth violence” (Ferguson, 2009).

Ultimately the responsibility of the actions of a person lie entirely with that person. I do not believe that the aggression exemplified in gaming is any different than any other aggression in life. The resulting actions from an aggressive build up is solely our decision, whether it be from gaming or friendly competition. We may find it convenient to displace the responsibility on to gaming, or guns, or television, but ultimately it is the decision of the individual that took action; an idea that may show more about our violent abilities than we are comfortable admitting.
