At present, many view artificial intelligence and its place in our future with distrust. They feel that somehow it may supplant us of our place at the top of the ecosystem we exist within, but the fear of technological advancement in the area of artificial intelligence is an unfounded one. Alan Kay, an award winning professor responsible for some of the greatest developments in computer science, puts this in perspective for us when he says, “Some people worry that artificial intelligence will make us feel inferior, but then, anybody in his right mind should have an inferiority complex every time he looks at a flower”. Though some regard the future of artificial intelligence with fear, it will prove to be a worthwhile and historical endeavor that will mark the beginning of a new age for humanity.

Currently, we define artificial intelligence (AI) as the ability of a computer or other machine to perform those activities that are normally thought to require intelligence (Casadey, 2004). Therefore, artificial intelligence can be classified as the programming logic a machine uses to characterize the environment around it. As Stanford University professor, Niles Nilsson (2005) describes, “The essence of a habile system is its ability to grow and change by learning, through imitation, experience, practice, and education-augmented by being reprogrammed when that is convenient”. We utilize such systems in fields such as medicine, education, robotics, engineering, mathematics, biology, the arts, language, law, architecture, commerce, space endeavors, and our military activities. In medicine, for example, the use of artificial intelligence helps by drawing from extensive databases to assist in prosthetic design and error prevention, employing learning algorithms which can track patients, helping to guide doctors and staff toward appropriate treatments. We also see a strong case for AI in military applications in which equipment can operate autonomouslyin hazardous areas and situations. This has saved lives in such activities as explosive ordnance disposal and drone reconnaissance missions. Mark Hansen, a director of the American Society of Safety Engineers, adds credibility to the usefulness of this technology in society stating that, “Computers are finally being used to do what they were originally designed to do – mundane, routine and sometimes hazardous tasks. As a result, computers are helping safety professionals protect people from injury and illness and, in the process, helping to improve quality of life” (p. 24). The use of artificial intelligence has even been used in the entertainment industry. Despite the practicality of the real world uses for this technology, we see it demonized in works such as iRobot, The Matrix, Transformers, The Terminator, Eagle Eye, Tron, and many more. Media seems to be the root of many of the fears we hold relevant for the progression of artificial intelligence design in the future, but unfortunately the more marketable option is one in which artificial intelligence is the protagonist. Counter to the beliefs of marketing analytics (another form of artificial intelligence by the way) for big budget movies, artificial intelligence is simply a branch of computer science and although popular science fiction may have us believe otherwise, it is nothing to fear.

Despite this rather simple definition, artificial intelligence has a storied history that lends itself as much to both the fantastical fiction created around it as it does the reality of the science. While the root of artificial intelligence goes back as far as we have been able to envision computation, the development of information-processing with which artificial intelligence and programming design draw heavily upon is credited to the work of a psychologist named Herb Simon (Buchanan, 2005). In 1944 Simon reasoned that, “Any rational decision may be viewed as a conclusion reached from certain premises… The behavior of a rational person can be controlled, therefore, if the value and factual premises upon which he bases his decisions are specified for him” (as cited in Buchanan, 2005). What Simon posits is that you can determine what someone will do if you look at their behavior as being made up of a series of variables that led him to that action. This definition shows how the abstract and seemingly indefinable nature of our thoughts, actions, and whims can correlate to the data of circumstances that made them up. Is not a bad day made up of many elements of bad experiences throughout the day? Is there not a reason behind every action? In the same way artificial intelligence is programmed with this cause and effect methodology in mind. This shows just how instrumental this development was for artificial intelligence theory. It is only in the last six and a half decades that we have been able to apply these ideas to actual technological tests through programming languages and other computational means.

As theory testing and research continued into the 1950’s and 60’s psychological principles remained important in the development of the science in such areas as knowledge representation; redefining the needs of intelligent design. During this time Universities such as the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Carnegie Mellon began to take interest in this broad field of advancement and opportunity. In the decades that followed, as technology became more complex and sophisticated, so too did the abilities of artificial intelligence. Development in AI research has taken us to new heights in its ability to understand through processes that are more natural and less linear and deductive. Today, we employ this kind of non-formal reasoning in programs to analyze data patterns utilizing such techniques as fuzzy logic and neural networks. With these advancements we are able to create learning algorithms, which categorize large sets of data to make decisions and predict outcomes. This decision-making capability extrapolated exponentially is what makes possible applications such as the self-driving cars being tested by Google and being made by University students competing in the Intelligent Ground Vehicle Competition. We have come a long way from simply theorizing how intelligent systems can think and predict. However, in a similar fashion to how we have developed from the infancy of logical design and computing, society still has a long way to go in its acceptance of the technology's place in the world. The idea that we will be replaced by mechanizations of our own creation stems from unsubstantiated claims of things yet to come with no grounding in historical facts. There is simply not enough evidence in the past or present that suggests the coming years will be anything but bright for the field of artificial intelligence in society.

In the future we can look forward to increased capabilities due to coming advancements in artificial intelligence. As we refine the processes and systems we use for machine learning, we can finally start to employ them in our everyday lives. Technology such as the Google self-driving car mentioned earlier are a great example of how our lives can be improved. This technology would drastically cut the amount of death and injury incurred in normal driving by removing human error, while at the same time allowing those who enjoy driving to continue to do so. The effects on industry do not stop at the introduction of a new way of travel though. As we start to automate the more mundane of human tasks with systems that can adapt to changes in the environment they were programmed for, we will enjoy an industrial boom of efficiency, job quality, and profits. Nils Nilson (2005) outlines his belief that there may be more to it than industrial efficiency saying that, “the performance of human jobs by machines might not, in the end, be as important as what the results of this research tell us about our own intelligence”. Nilson sees the technology as a way to study ourselves through emulation of our own abilities, but he also relays a concern many express over the automation of industry and the replacement of human jobs due to this automation. This worry that can be traced back to a fundamental fear of the unknown, coupled with a technology that already far surpasses our ability; a fear that is not as relevant as one may think. While it is undeniable that the current positions some workers currently hold would be replaced by autonomous processes, there is a bigger picture to be seen. The machines and artificial intelligence systems would need a wealth of maintainers for functions that the machine could not possibly account for itself, suggesting a need for the creation of new jobs of a higher quality and satisfaction than those that would be replaced. David Rotman (2013) explains how this is something that has been exhibited in industry since before the 1700’s:
>"improvements in technology have changed the nature of work and destroyed some types of jobs in the process. In 1900, 41 percent of Americans worked in agriculture; by 2000, it was only 2 percent. Likewise, the proportion of Americans employed in manufacturing has dropped from 30 percent in the post–World War II years to around 10 percent today—partly because of increasing automation, especially during the 1980s"

We will continue to eliminate jobs as we become more efficient and technologically advanced; eventually accepting our place in the future and adapting to the new environments we live in. One example of this progression of acceptance can be found in the need to grade educational testing. As the use of standardized testing grew, so too did the need for all those tests to be graded; a task that traditionally would require a large amount of manpower to accomplish. Instead, many educational testing companies who were awarded the contracts, began to use automated forecasting techniques such as machine scoring to keep up with the contracts that they were responsible for (Mayes, 2014). This is not a new concept to industry or a new fear, it is a recurring theme in our struggle to define ourselves in the face of technological and societal change. A change which is representative of our growth as a species of aware and intelligent beings. A change that the furthering of artificial intelligence research and application will make inevitable. Alex Hern (2014), a technology reporter for The Guardian, believes that, “On aggregate level, these changes are a good thing. If the work of millions of people is covered by algorithms, then output goes up, hours worked go down, and we move one step closer to a Jetsons-style utopia”. This lies in direct contrast to the idea that the progression of artificial intelligence research need be a linear ranking with that of human progression. Instead it helps construct the idea that both can develop in parallel to each other and may even help each other reach new heights in that progression. There is a place for everybody in the world of tomorrow – a new age in which an enormous amount of opportunity is possible. Such innovation marks our twenty-first century equivalent to the industrial revolution; bringing about a change in the way we communicate with each other, experience the world around us, and think about our futures. It can lead to a never before experienced level of freedom in which we are able to use this tech to reach ever higher aspirations for mankind.

As artificial intelligence continues to improve our lives by becoming smarter in how it interacts and understands the world around it, so too will we grow to understand ourselves. Such innovation provides a platform for a future not even science fiction can fully predict. Still, we have a long way to go before such an ideal world is a reality. Any great innovation comes with a healthy amount of growing pains. These pains are simply indication that we are in fact growing. The real cancer is the fear mongering and stagnation that comes with refusing technological growth. Artificial intelligence today is regarded with all suspicion and fear that science once was; heralded as the black magic and wizardry that would undo societies and end mankind. Likewise, we will look upon our understanding of it with the same humorous light with which we see those early days. There is nothing evil or bewitching about the design of artificial intelligence.
