Information has been a valuable and traded commodity for as long as humans have been able to communicate with one another. It has started wars, inspired innovation, ended lives, and toppled governments. So why is it that we are only now realizing its personal worth to us? Is it perhaps that the threat of it no longer being personal has rekindled our desire to keep it sacred?

>“They who can give up essential liberty to obtain a little temporary safety deserve neither liberty nor safety” - Benjamin Franklin

On one side of the argument, we have those concerned with maintaining independence, anonymity, and privacy online. On the other, those who want to prey on the information for capitalist gain and marketing ploys. The debate rages on as policy continues to shape an oppressive martial law style landscape where control and safety of our information is uncertain. How do we enjoy freedom from the misuse of information gathered on us without harming the free speech, community, and expansive reach we now enjoy? It is quite simple actually. We have to first recognize and educate ourselves on what is actually happening behind the scenes. No product is free, no service without sacrifice. We all pay. If not with money, then in some other way. Markets are built and ruined by the participation and investment of people. Do you think Facebook would last very long if no one logged in anymore? What do you think would happen if Google was no longer the de facto search engine? What if everyone used [Duck Duck Go](https://www.duckduckgo.com) instead?

 Without the participation of the users companies have nothing to sell. “What’s important to remember in this discussion is that we are not their customers. We are their product. Or, rather, when we use their services for free, information about our habits, friends, and preferences becomes their product, which they then sell to the companies of the Digital Advertising Alliance—their customers.” says Brenden Greeley (2012) in his Bloomberg Businessweek article. And you know what, he is absolutely right. We are being mined for information under the guise of being provided a service. It is what these companies and services do with our information afterward that really shows where the future of this business is.

 The war over information is nothing that is new to humanity, but what is new is the attitude of users in today’s social climate. We need to be more careful, clear, and critical than we currently are online. Though you may be logging in from the safety, comfort, and privacy of your own home, you are entering a world with little rules where nothing you do is ever completely safe from both the most innocent of onlookers and the most nefarious of criminals; including those in the well established companies we so easily trust.

>"The survival of American democracy depends as much on the defense of liberty as it does on the defense of our physical security.

 All in all it is on us to make the decision of what and how much we give up about our data as well as how that data is used. We should be abstaining from applications, services, and companies that do not have our best interest at heart and making intelligent decisions about the form of communication we use to share data with one another. Or perhaps take the stance CNN author Frida Ghitis (2012) suggests, “The only effective way to change the ways of these giant corporations -- and the smaller ones following the same practices -- is by pushing the government to make those practices illegal”. The problem with this approach is we often keep getting pushed back to bills and legislation that favor control and restriction of the users like. Speaking to that effect, CNN writer Rebecca MacKinnon (2012) argues, “The advent of the Internet does not change a bedrock truth upon which our nation was founded: The survival of American democracy depends as much on the defense of liberty as it does on the defense of our physical security. Those two ideals always have been and always will be in tension; but without a healthy balance between them -- online as well as offline -- we cannot live free of fear.”

 Simply grumbling and continuing to give the thief what he wants is not going to make him go away. We need to actively police these companies and know what it is we are agreeing to by using their services. Benjamin Franklin never spoke more relevant words than when we said, “They who can give up essential liberty to obtain a little temporary safety deserve neither liberty nor safety”. What do you, my dear readers, think about the future outcome of this war on society? Is there cause for concern? How can we find the happy medium between big business/government practices and personal information security?
