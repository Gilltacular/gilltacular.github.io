The credit card industry is provides an amazing opportunity through the loan service it offers to consumers. However, due to regulatory neglect and in some cases down right fraud, this convenience and opportunity we embrace is in a deplorable state. Credit cards are not in and of themselves evil. It is the mismanagement on all sides that allows what could be a healthy credit ecosystem to thrive off deceit and baleful intent.

The credit card industry came to fruition in the 1980s; a time when Citibank and the state of  South Dakota were going broke. Citibank needed more money than they were allowed to charge their customers in interest in order to stay in the black. The problem for South Dakota was that the products they exported were not making up for the cost of the expenses it took to ship them. With such a dire outlook for both the state and Citibank, something had to be done.

Citibank had a mutually beneficial answer for them. Citibank would create enough jobs to supplement the state of South Dakota if they, in turn, passed legislation that let them operate from their state. The state of South Dakota agreed and once adopted, this legislation created a snowball effect as other states such as Delaware started to latch on to the idea. The age of the credit card had began. Robin stein described the boon by saying:

>“Between 1980 and 1990, the number of credit cards more than doubled, credit card spending increased more than five-fold and the average household credit card balance rose from $518 to nearly $2,700. With the cost of money sinking and average balances climbing, profits soared.”

This whole situation was also helped by President Carter, who as part of his work to lower inflation instituted a freeze on credit accounts. It was a short termed freeze, but that was long enough for credit card companies to start charging an annual fee without garnering heat from their consumers. These annual fees quickly became a large source of profit for the credit card companies and offset the costs of some of the less profitable consumers; the responsible ones who paid their bill in full monthly.

The credit card industry is one that takes advantage of those who cannot afford to be taken advantage of. The ideal customer, referred to as revolvers, pays off credit cards in a timeframe that requires them to pay an increased interest rate on the borrowed amount. Penalty interest rates, fees, and a system that does not promote paying on time all are evidence to this idea. For many people in the United States, credit and credit cards are a never ending cycle that can only be escaped by a bankruptcy filing.

Credit cards are marketed to many Americans by outlining the attractions of buying things you otherwise would not be able to afford. Outlining the benefits of lower minimum payments coupled with higher borrowing amounts without giving the same emphasis to the fees, interest, and other penalties. The marketing of credit cards in the United States is based on the idea that people will not do their research to find out the important information regarding the financial contracts they are signing. A perfect example of this is the introductory offers sent out by credit cards. A near meaningless offer that will very likely never be taken advantage of that is designed to target and in some instances trick consumers.

In addition, the contract sent with credit cards is one that violates the very premise of contract law which is described by Professor Elizabeth Warren as, “the two parties to the contract understand what the terms are”. It is one of the only loaning institutions that can change the terms of the agreement you make with them and grandfather the new terms to the money you have borrowed under the previous terms and in nearly all cases require only fifteen days notice to do so, leaving you with little recourse but to pay the amount owed in full within that time before the change or live with the new terms. While they may be flying under the radar of the law, they do so in ways that allow them ambiguity of terms in order to take advantage of borrowers. The credit card companies use the inadequacies of the law to create what Warren describes as “traps”. Such traps as Universal default, which allows the credit card companies to change the terms of your agreement based on changes in your credit worthiness due to such instances as not making a payment on any other lender’s loan with you.

Legislation that has allowed freedom of market pricing, like Smiley Vs City Bank and the Market decision before it, has only increased the amount of leeway credit card institutions have in raising fees and types of fees they can charge. These penalties are seen by the credit card companies as a profit stream but marketed as a penalty for violating contract terms. Contract terms that the banks themselves are not liable to hold themselves to as they can change them with little notice.

Regulation for these banks falls to the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (the O.C.C.). This is a washington agency under the Office of Treasury that regulates the banks that issue credit cards. This regulation is based around three major goals, “Make sure the banks do not fail. To ensure the integrity of how the banks operate their corporate governance, and to make sure they deal fairly and honestly with their customers” (Bergman, 2004). The O.C.C. claims the power to take action against companies when they violate any of these tenants. Such action can be seen from the example of Providian, who were targeting customers who were high risk and purposefully holding customer's payments for a time in order to make income on fees from these crossed charges. In this situation, the O.C.C. intervened in such a way that the case was resolved in court to the favor of the people. In many cases though, the state Attorney General and District Attorneys allege that the O.C.C. claimed jurisdiction over their cases, acknowledged the unfair practices of the banks in these credit card systems, but did nothing to stop them. Williams reinforces the power the O.C.C. has by saying, “If we had a basis for concluding that a bank was involved in a practice that was unfair or deceptive, if it violated any of the other many consumer protection standards that applied to them, we can tell them to stop it immediately” but this is not something that Professor Warren believes they will do. She states her opposition by stating:

>“Oh, the federal regulators have been very active recently. But the way they've been active is, they have been suing the state regulators to tell them to keep their hands off the credit card companies. In other words, our regulators are there, many of them. ... [But] most of them are political appointees, and most of them see their principal job as protecting the safety and soundness of the banks, and that means seeing that the banks are profitable. Their customer is not the American family; their customer is the profitable bank that issues credit cards. That's who they are designed to protect, and that's who they are protecting”.

This distrustful behavior is not lost on the public though. The better business bureau states that credit card companies are number one in the amount of complaints they receive out of the one thousand different industries they track. Pat Wallace of the Bay area Better Business Bureau states,

>“These things are not an anomaly, all these complaints have some basis in a fact. There are irritated, dissatisfied customers in this industry”.

Connecticut Senator Chris Dodd believes the reason that reform has not been productive in its attempt to stop these bad business practices. He states that

>“Every time we have tried to offer legislation, this industry has become very very powerful and it is very successful in defeating every legislative attempt that has been made over the last several years to inject some responsibility on the part of the credit card industry”.

I quite seriously believe that this is a system that promoted bad business practice as a means to profit from the confusion and inadequacies of its consumers. I also believe that it falls under the responsibility of the business to provide clear concise terms in a contract agreement that they cannot change without the explicit consent of the consumer in a new agreement. That it is the government's responsibility to protect the citizens and consumers from businesses that abuse their power by regulation and reform. If neither work to correct the actions of the company then it is their responsibility to bring to light the parties responsible for the corruption and either dismantle the organization or take punitive action against the guilty.

On the other hand, we cannot solely blame the O.C.C., the Attorney General, or even the credit card companies themselves for the way things are. It also lies on our shoulders as consumers to both make educated decisions in our purchasing and financial transactions as well as bring up any complaint we have of unfair practices to the government organizations. It is a cycle where each and every part of the ecosystem does not fully do its job to prevent injustice. Therefore, the so called ‘lowest man on the totem pole’ is hurt. The blame can go to consumer education, government, credit card companies and on and on but ultimately the problem will only be solved when people stand up and educate themselves about the decisions they make.

Like in South Dakota, credit cards provide a service that when used properly can create great opportunity where it may not have existed otherwise. They are a tool and like any proper tool must be respected as such. There is no magic in finances. Think of the many innovations we have today. How many nearly failed due to improper investments, funding, or support. Credit cards can provide that support. This is an age where we do not necessarily need to be rich to fund your own endeavors. Where you do not need to convince an investment board that what you have is worthy of money before you can create it. This is a time where a single person with a great idea has the option to risk it all on that idea and work doing everything they can to accomplish it. There is no reason why credit cards and the companies that create them need to diminish that idea with shady business practices.
