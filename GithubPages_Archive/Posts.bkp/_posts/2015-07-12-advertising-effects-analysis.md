Social Psychology is described as the scientific study of how people think about, influence, and relate to each other (Peplau, Taylor, & Sears, 2006). It may not be a surprise given the nature of human ambition that the findings of these studies have been leveraged to take advantage of and exploit the way we interact with each other in order to obtain greater profit. This has never been more prevalent than it is in advertising. So prevalent, in fact, that we assume it is simply a social norm in today’s current climate that we are so bombarded with salience and attempts to manipulate the impressions and attributions we make. If truly we are, even in some small part, as Sigmund Freud once theorized, a product of our environment, then it is of grave importance that we analyze our environment and the tactics used to shape it such as those practiced in advertising and reporting to ensure that it is a healthy one. It is in this spirit that I take a look at the common practices of advertising and marketing.

>"We are an ever evolving machine of stimuli processing prowess that can evaluate, categorize, average, and process information on the fly."

We make determinations about the things we interact with in life in a variety of ways. So many ways, in fact, that no single practice or endeavor could ever hope to dominate them all as quickly as we can come up with ways to adapt to them. We are an ever evolving machine of stimuli processing prowess that can evaluate, categorize, average, and process information on the fly. Unfortunately, one of the great flaws of this incredible machine we call the human mind is the interdependence we exhibit within our social structures. This is what allows us to shape our identity within an environment, learn from other people’s experiences, and assume information with incredible accuracy. It also enables us to be manipulated by others, deceived, and is a driving force behind the behavior to automatically accept social roles without proper evaluation.

We are not solely the sum of our parts though. We exhibit a much slower changing psychological base behavior that ties directly to our survival. We have and continue to develop what is called evolved psychological mechanisms, or human tendencies and preferences that result from natural selection (as cited in Social Psychology, 2006, p.13). These are the core survival behaviors that have been passed down through our social and hereditary ancestry. This instinctual response, along with the development of our schema for individual circumstances, helps us avoid such social dangers as ostracism and plays a vital part in influencing our self image. We readily reject ideas that challenge these inherent evolved mechanisms and often use what is called the primacy effect, in which the use of an initial impression to interpret subsequent information is utilized to make relevant schema in order to avoid any other subsequent potentially dangerous ideas from taking hold again (Peplau, et al., 2006).

>"If we so readily reject notions that are dangerous to us and we are assuming advertising is indeed also bad for us, do we not simply adapt to it and ignore its effects in same way we do with other ideas?"

A very valid question to ask at this point would be: why then, if we so readily reject notions that are dangerous to us and we are assuming advertising is indeed also bad for us, do we not simply adapt to it and ignore its effects in same way we do with other ideas? The answer to this is in how we relate to the information being given. Typically the years of repetition in responding to like situations in our environment have made our emotional, cognitive, and behavioral responses to many situations automatic (as cited in Social Psychology, 2006, p. 73). This is known in social psychology as automatic evaluation and plays a large part in the shaping of our ideals by mass media and advertising. As we grow up and more stimuli is processed we develop schema. These schema start to broaden as we get older and we start to apply more and more of our experiences to them. Instead of developing new feelings, opinions, and categorization to ideas we more readily apply the previous schema we have adopted much like a filter for the new information. This automatic evaluation skips the reason processing and critical analysis steps almost entirely and over time can lead to the adoption of wildly different ideals and views than we originally held. Most of the time this automatic evaluation is harmless and serves to free up our mind from commonly used tasks that typically arrive at the same conclusion, but these mental shortcuts, known as heuristics, are also one of the greater liabilities that make us vulnerable to such strategies as are deployed in marketing and advertising.

Our identity also plays a large part in how we react to and use information and the shaping of our ideals and goals also known as our self-concept (Peplau, et al., 2006). By constructing a social norm for our ideal self, or the personal attributes we would like to have (Peplau, et al., 2006), advertising techniques focus on manipulating our ought self, the personal attributes we believe we should possess (Peplau, et al., 2006). Our social identity is further shaped by the influence such advertising has on those around us as well. We may initially reject an idea, but give it more credence when we discover that people who we identify with and respect may have a different view than us on the matter. This social comparison is a staple of our self perception. In addition, when others are then in turn influenced by our acceptance of such ideals a snowball effect starts to form and social norms are manipulated to include the new standards.

Every day you are likely assaulted by a deluge of marketing that is particularly targeted at the modification of our self schema to generalize and better react to products. What use is there for beauty products, for instance, if you already think you are beautiful without them? So instead of directly targeting you by saying that you are not beautiful and risking the triggering of an evolved psychological mechanism that would reject such stimuli from taking hold, advertising instead focuses on manipulating what the social norms for the ideal self are. They focus on comparisons you make against what social psychology refers to as your possible self, or the schema people hold concerning what they may or could become in the future (Peplau, et al., 2006). By doing this they effectively trigger a state of self-awareness where suddenly you are considering whether you are beautiful or not, this shortcut to your self schema completely bypasses the normal processing and analyzation that would normally take place, allowing for automatic evaluation of primed information designed to make you more accepting of their message. Advertising, marketing, and the media in general have evolved from simply reporting facts about a situation to targeted ads that compare unrealistic ideals and truths in order to manipulate the way you feel about things.

>"We have developed into a society with instant communication and information so quickly that we have had to shortcut the processing and keep up."

Such rapid acceptance of social norms has never been as prevalent a problem as it is today; a time in which the speed of sharing information and the wide collection of data on people’s behaviors have created a perfect climate for a society that responds to stimuli faster than we can process it. In another time a person would have periods in which such ideas could process in their due course and the process of information adoption was slower, allowing information to spread and proper evidence to present itself in corroboration or objection to the claims being made. This allowed the idea of social norms to ebb and flow naturally as we processed the information being given individually against our own ideals and beliefs rather than rocket forward with no regard to long term effect as it does today. We have developed into a society with instant communication and information so quickly that we have had to shortcut the processing to keep up. Without diligent effort toward the giving of time to study and process information we have become a society of individuals with a generalist belief that is easy to predict and market to. Many times we conform to social norms so readily out of instinct that we simply adopt the widely used norms as our schema, letting popular opinion, the media, and other vested interests control the sway of our opinions and feelings.

You may ask how it can be possible that someone or even some team of people could create such a structure that they are able to convince a large majority of people of something without regard to whether the truth supports their claims or not. To answer this question we turn to Social Psychology (Peplau, Sears & Taylor, 2006) which describes an idea called transfer of effect. That is, a changing of attitude by transferring to it the affect associated with another object, or more simply, pairing something appealing with another idea in order for the appealing effect to elevate the inclination you have toward the other thing. This is one of the earliest and base ideas in marketing and advertising. You can see evidence in everything from store advertisements to magazine and news covers which like to pair beautiful women with outlandish claims of weight loss and medications with pictures of families who are happy and smiling. Though seemingly innocent in presentation, such ideas can cloud our expectations of ourselves and others by presenting a false world that is more desirable than the one we currently live in. There was so much of an effect from this type of marketing that the tobacco industry was banned from advertising cigarettes on television and radio in the US by congress with the institution of the Public Health Cigarette Smoking Act and tightened its reigns even further later with amendments of the Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act (Federal Trade Commission, 2010). Though the cigarette companies themselves are not the only ones using tactics of misrepresentation to push their message.

>"This power to create good or bad associations in lieu of the reality of a situation must be reined in if we are to ever want for a true analysis of the world we live in and decisions we make."

Take the seemingly transparent advertisement ran in newspaper and printed magazines in 2005, run by the Cancer Patient Aid Association. An advertisement which portrays Hitler smoking a pipe with two small word pairs, one next to the pipe and the other bordering Hitler’s likeness, accompany the image in a minimalist fashion reading “53 million” and “6 million” respectively. The image features dark shadows around Hitler's figure, accentuating a foreboding and maleficent intent. The color choices and scowl encourage negative emotion in the audience’s perception of the character and the tone of the message itself. The character’s face, pipe, and the brim of his hat stand out the most; possibly to draw attention to who the character is and his affiliation. Yet the brightness in Hitler’s face could be adding just enough humanity to remain relatable to the audience. These intentional elements set the stage for interpretation of the message they wish to convey. A message that implies smoking is deadlier than Hitler. Advertisements put out by anti-smoking advocates often use visually striking images to incite emotional response and make comparisons that otherwise may be unrelated. This kind of advertising is just as damaging as that of the corporate body they are speaking against; the tobacco companies. In an attempt to emphasize the correlation between the negative aspects of the symbols and that of their message this advertising technique ignores logical association. Or more specifically, the correlation between the deaths represented by each symbol in the advertisement should not imply that that they are relationally significant or that one is a good measure of the other. While this tactic strengthens the perception of smoking as evil and deadly, by showing the large difference in death count, it also serves to demean the original connotation of the symbol it makes comparison to; that of Hitler and the atrocities of World War II. It improperly draws on the idea of equality between the actions of a Nazi leader whose acts left no room for choice in those he affected, to something that is as avoidable as exercising personal restraint. In effect, this advertisement serves to promotes illogical and unsubstantiated claims in advertising and reasoning.

These tactics in advertising hold no regard for the logical fallacy it commits by comparing two unlike things as if they were relative to one another. It crafts an ideology that obscures the lines of logic to incite reaction. Such systems of delivery can have undesired consequences on the way we view important aspects of our past as well as the world around us and can have widespread repercussions in society as the long term effects shape what we consider social norms and the connections we make with various ideas and stimuli, also known as association (Peplau. et al., 2006). It can also be seen in such studies as those done by Mohammad Reza Dalvi and Mina Vahidi (2013) which concluded that "marketing activities improve organization’s competitiveness and also increase socially learned behaviors among them through encouraging and motivating employees”. This power to create good or bad associations in lieu of the reality of a situation must be reined in if we are to ever have a true analysis of the world we live in and the decisions we make.

>"No longer does a monopolized media hub make sense now that different countries and societies, each with their own ideals, beliefs, and learned associations are connected as if neighbors in the same culdasack."

Interestingly a greater push for unedited and less bias information has taken to one of the only places such an endeavor could thrive, the internet. Media services such as LiveLeak, that host videos taken by users without editing, as well as a greater connection to reporting from non profit interests and people who live in the countries events being portrayed in the news takes place has grown. More and more as a people we are coming to understand that when it comes to information gathering we need to process information by its parts instead of simply seeing one perspective on the matter. We are adapting somewhat to the constant stimulus presented to us; using each source as a element of a larger picture through the interconnected framework the internet provides. It is the beginning of another phase of evolution for us as we try to adapt to the quickly changing world we live in. No longer does a monopolized media hub make sense now that different countries and societies, each with their own ideals, beliefs, and learned associations are connected as if neighbors in the same culdasack. The gap between the events that happen half a world away has shortened from tens of thousands of miles away to the distance between us and our keyboards. With this change comes a need to study the techniques and failings of advertising and media historically so as not to repeat our acceptance of them in the future.

In conclusion, we as a society are generally desensitized to the immoral psychological practices that advertising and journalism uses to present information to us. If we are to create unbiased and correct schemas for newly presented information, we must actively sensitize ourselves to the need to check information against the common practices these industries employ for their own benefit in order to gain any real understanding of the issues and facts. For this sensitization to happen, we must either take on the effort personally, or we must demand analyzation of the morality of such techniques of presenting information and reformation of those practices. This relatively new world of information at our fingertips is an unwritten chapter for which the author is yet to be decided. We can either let the chapter be written for us and possibly lose our part in the story or take hold of the words being put to the page and write our future purposefully.
