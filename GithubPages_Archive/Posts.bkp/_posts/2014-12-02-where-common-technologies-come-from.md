The Trans-Atlantic cable is a sub-marine stretch of cable spanning the Atlantic ocean for means of international communication between North America and Europe. It was a remarkable feat of endurance that though many attempts and an extraordinary amount or perseverance came to fruition.

The father of this great invention was a man named Cyrus Field, the son of an important clergyman. Field co-owned a paper company and by the time he was near thirty years of age was quite wealthy. Having excelled in his business enterprise, he searched for a new project to undertake. He found one such project when his brother introduced him to Frederick Gisborne, owner of a telegraph company, who proposed a plan to build a telegraph that would connect America to Europe in communication and shorten the time to send messages by nearly two weeks. Field was extremely interested in the proposal and started work right away. He gathered financial support from wealthy investors who made up the ‘Cable Cabinet’ and pledged one and half million dollars towards the endeavor.

Due to improper expectations as to what to expect for a project of this magnitude, the beginnings of the cable took nearly a year longer to complete than expected and Field was soon out of money. Not one to be dissuaded by setbacks, Field traveled to Europe to raise more money from financial backers there, including the British Foreign Secretary of the time, Lord Clarendon. His determination to succeed and confident manner won him the financing he needed to continue. By 1857 Field had the cable constructed and was ready to start laying. A mere 200 miles off the coast, another bleak turn stunted Field’s progress and patience when during the laying process the cable snapped. It took until the next year in 1858 for the cable to be replaced and a new expedition to be set up. This time a storm hit, only 100 miles offshore and nearly destroyed the boat, snapping the cable a second time. Later that year on the third expedition in this now seemingly improbable endeavor Field, according to PBS online (2004), remained hopeful stating: "When I thought of all that we had passed through, of the hopes thus far disappointed, of the friends saddened by our reverses.... I felt a load at my heart almost too heavy to bear." He wasn't ready to give up, though, "My confidence was firm and my determination fixed."

A month later the cable was successfully laid and Field was finally able to return home to growing excitement towards the inception of this new technology that had been such a bear to get running. All that excitement ended when anticlimactically the signal failed upon testing of the transmission. It was not until a few years later in 1865, that Field was able to mount another expedition to try again. This attempt mirrored that of the other early endeavors abruptly failing. Yet Field remained undaunted and in 1866 the second transatlantic cable was successfully laid.
