In this time of rapid technological advancement where there is a device every few months that totes a new set of features and designs, it can be hard to imagine what is next for the advent of personal technology. What big breakthrough will change the industry next? From collegiate level professors to multi-billion dollar corporations, this question is studied and asked over and over again. The answer seems elusive, yet many researchers believe that at least one of these budding advancements will be in the field of virtual retinal technology.

Virtual retinal technology is a subset of wearable computing that has to do specifically with enhancing the information available to us by utilizing how we perceive objects through vision. The idea is to create a form of augmentation for reading and displaying information that is beamed directly onto either a small reflective screen just within your peripheral view or onto the eye itself. The University of Washington's Human Interface Technology Laboratory (Tidwell, Johnston, Melville, & Furness, n.d.) describes this best:
>"In a conventional display a real image is produced. The real image is either viewed directly or, as in the case with most head-mounted displays, projected through an optical system and the resulting virtual image is viewed. The projection moves the virtual image to a distance that allows the eye to focus comfortably." (para 3.1)
This is all done with a device that has the computing power of a smart phone stuffed into the entirety of it's chassis. The device can take a multitude of commands. Everything from voice recognition and adaptive touch controls to reading the movements of the eye can be use to interface with this incredible piece of technology.

Though beaming a gauntlet of lasers directly into your eye may seem dangerous, the technology proposed modifies the beam to disperse over an area that John R. Lewis (2004) describes as “hundreds of thousands of times larger than a single spot of an incident beam” (para. 7). Since this laser is no longer concentrated on a single location, but instead is a scanned beam diluted over the entire surface of the eye, it poses no more harm to the eye than any other artificial light source would.

The actual capabilities, form factor, and overall design of individual virtual retinal display devices change depending on the application they are designed for. Their various features and functionality are specifically tailored to a certain job. The difference between this and a traditional computer is as Mann (1998) states:
>"The assumption of wearable computing is that the user will be doing something else at the same time as doing the computing. Thus the computer should serve to augment the intellect, or augment the senses." (Operational Modes of Wearable Computing section, para. 1)
This idea of multitasking and enhancement has remained constant over the development of virtual retinal technology in the fifteen years since this was written. The idea that the technology we create no longer needs to be the obstacle we must overcome and adapt to, but instead we can be free to focus on the the interactions of being human and let the interface augment our abilities making it natural to fulfill them with minimal effort, training, or risk to the user.

The vast potential for this technology is being quickly recognized in many professional areas as well. Surgeons may use the device to view an overlay of vitals being updated to a heads up display without ever having to look up from their patient. This kind of device provides a platform to increase precision, quality, and efficiency of the user's work. Or as Mann (2013) suggests, this same doctor may be able to see what is happening to the patient at the time a problem was first detected, using the device as a sort of 'black box' for health issues. The practical applications are endless and will continue to change the way we interact with our machines, as well as how we interact with each other.

Google Glass is another great example of how a device utilizing virtual retinal technology can enhance our interactions with not only ourselves, but also with each other. Devices such as these could be used like that of what Mann (2001) describes, a world in which technologies such as automatic face recognition give an individual the ability to check if the person knocking on their door is really who they say they are. Perhaps someone on their way home may use a device such as Google Glass, to navigate through traffic, or to message their significant other, without ever having to take their hands off the wheel of their vehicle. The varied use of virtual retinal technology has been an exploration decades in the making and one that is just starting to bloom. It will not be long before the social and professional norms catch up with the science behind such devices and start to capitalize on the plethora of uses that this technology provides.

No matter the applied use, virtual retinal displays remain an extension of the human mind. It augments our perception of the world by providing data we can clearly, immediately, and privately visualize in a way that does not intrude on our natural actions, enhancing our experience and work in phenomenal ways without ever tying us to a physical location. As Paul Miller (2012) writes in a recent article regarding this budding new technology:
>"Once upon a time, in MIT's storied hallways, young men and women envisioned a future where a computer was subtler. Where it 'got out of your way,' as Google puts it. The idea was that a computer could no longer call you away from the real world and into its hunched ergonomics, but instead it would make you a better part of that real world, head up and smarter than ever" (wrap up section, para 3).
As this technology continues to advance so too will our capabilities as the individual and ambitious beings we are.
