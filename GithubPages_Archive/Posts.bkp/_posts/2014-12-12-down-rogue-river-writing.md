I dug the oar into the water, paddling ineptly as the cold mountain river rushed around me. Adrenaline trickled into my bloodstream and my focus began to waver. I felt the water’s icy touch on my skin, barely noticing it in the moment, my blood pressure soaring beyond the level of medical recommendation. “Shit!”, I heard my voice cry in fear as I saw the class three rapid ahead swallow my buddy Josh and shoot him out the other side. If the four hour car ride to get here wasn’t indication enough, I now knew there was no turning back.

The river was bringing me closer to the narrowed banks that created the first rapid on the thirty-mile stretch we had planned out; the first I had ever encountered. It egged me on in that mocking manner that you imagine a schoolyard bully would, shoving and pushing until you have no choice but to act. I steadied my breath and tried to recall the exact maneuvers Josh had taken when he went through, hoping mimicry of his successful attempt would be my salvation. The way he dug his oar into the water to position himself in the current and how he seemed to paddle for dear life on the white crusted crest of each wave so as not to get caught. Josh was a five foot some odd inch Eagle Scout with knowledge of the water and the experience and comfort of his own kayak under him. I knew I could trust his example, but was not confident in my ability to mimic it on my own attempt. In the starkest of contrasts, I stood a six foot three bearded man stuffed into four feet of space, with no experience on the river, and having never even seen an inflatable kayak before this trip; I was in no way mentally prepared for the sheer panic I was experiencing coming up to the first rapid of many this journey would require.

The rapids ahead were unavoidable now. They created a strong flow that pushed and tugged at my kayak trying to flip me around backwards as if adamant in its belief that going forwards through the them wasn’t intimidating enough. As I came into the first chute I marveled for a microsecond at the power of the river around me. I listened as a stampede of water roared and crashed around like medieval warhorses crashing through the front-lines of an army. I could have never anticipated the emotions that sound would incite. Seemingly my body and mind knew the risk this activity posed to my existence and resisted it as if will were enough to make it untrue. I had no experience on the river, but something primal understood the situation I was in. The channel was the width of a small truck, but may as well of been the ocean from the deafening sonance that enveloped my senses. I let go of life at that moment, giving up to the whims of the river. I was prepared for the worst and in that preparation accepted the possibility of my death as an outcome of this venture.

As soon as I let go all fear vanished. It was as if accepting my fate brought me solace and clarity to move with decisiveness and intention. Strength found my limbs as I stabbed at the water with my oar, holding it fast I braced against the powerful flow of water rushing around me in order to make one last corrective maneuver before I was at the mercy of the tumultuous battlefield that lay before me. My enemy, the river, responded by slamming against the side of my kayak shoving me helplessly sideways as I met the gate of the rapids. For a small moment I didn’t know what was happening. All I knew was that I was moving over the first and easiest part of the rapid ahead in the worst position possible. No movement of my oar in the water would right me. I scooped sideways over the first wave just to the right of a small rock sitting unseen in the white foam of the rapid, also known as the hair. The river swept me helplessly downstream.

It was as if time was broken. I would move a hundred miles an hour for a few milliseconds and then as I barrelled over another rapid it would all but stop, the spray of water hanging in the air for a moment before shooting in all directions like the ground during an artillery strike called in with me as the target. I spun completely backwards as the river rushed horizontally from the bank shoving me back into the main traffic of water. I pushed my oar down again, the current grasping at it with a steel grip as if to disarm me of my only weapon in the fight. I pulled my oar above the reach of the water’s grasp and I was again facing forward. Time returned to its normal momentum as the cacophony of aqueous battle dissipated behind me and I moved into the pool ahead.

Josh was ahead of me, lazily paddling upstream to maintain his position as he waited for my approach. “So,” he questioned loudly when I was close enough, “how was it?”. I corrected again slightly, pushing out of the main current and toward a calm spot next to a sandbar ahead. “That was rough!” I yelled in a much more exasperated tone than anticipated. “What’s the next one look like?” I added in an attempt to regain some bit of face after my previous admission of intimidation. “We have a few more like that and then one that is one magnitude larger after”, Josh replied as he studied the guide. My mind swam at the thought of what would constitute a magnitude greater than what I had just experienced. “Well” I said in a hopeless sort of laughter, “there is no turning back now I guess”. With that we moved down the river once again, a little more experienced and feeling more alive than ever before. This was going to be an amazing trip.
